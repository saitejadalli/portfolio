Professional bio

I am Saiteja Dalli, and I am working in data analytics. I am interested in how data becomes a decision, and in the tools, increasingly powered by AI and machine learning, that analysts use to get there.

To use those tools well, I want to understand them. For my first portfolio artifact I researched the history of AI and machine learning, from the Turing Test in 1950 to generative and agentic systems in 2026, and organised it into a visual timeline that a non-specialist can follow.

That project shows how I like to work: gather reliable sources, find the pattern in a large body of information, and present it clearly for a specific audience.

I am aiming for a data analyst role where I can turn information into clear, trustworthy insight, and where I keep learning as the tools change.

A1

AI and machine learning timeline, 1950–2026

Introduction

AI tools are now part of everyday analytics work, but they did not appear overnight. This artifact traces how the field got here, from the Turing Test to generative and agentic AI.

Description

A five-slide visual history of artificial intelligence and machine learning. It covers major milestones, influential researchers and technologies, the two AI winters and what ended them, where AI is making a difference today, and the sources behind the facts.

Objective

Show that progress in AI was not a straight line, and explain what data, algorithms and computing power made possible, so that a non-specialist can judge today's tools with more confidence.

Tools and technologies used

  • PowerPoint
  • Desk research
  • Timeline design
  • Source citation

Process

  1. Collected the major milestones from 1950 to 2026 and the researchers behind them.
  2. Arranged them into four eras: early symbolic rules, statistical learning, deep neural networks, then large-scale generative and agentic systems.
  3. Examined the first AI winter (1974–80) and second AI winter (1987–93): their causes, their impact, and what enabled the revival.
  4. Mapped real-world uses across healthcare, finance, agriculture, transport, education and work, and energy and industry.
  5. Added a reference slide listing leading researchers, AI companies and labs, and linked sources.

Key milestones

  1. 1950Turing Test: Alan Turing proposes the imitation game as a way to discuss machine intelligence.
  2. 1956Dartmouth: John McCarthy and colleagues establish AI as a formal research field.
  3. 1959Machine learning: Arthur Samuel popularises the term with a self-improving checkers program.
  4. 1974–80First AI winter: funding and optimism decline as early systems fall short of ambitious promises.
  5. 1986Backpropagation: Hinton, Rumelhart and Williams help popularise training multilayer neural networks.
  6. 1987–93Second AI winter: expert-system limits and a market downturn reduce investment and confidence.
  7. 1997Deep Blue: IBM's chess computer defeats world champion Garry Kasparov.
  8. 2012AlexNet: deep convolutional networks trained on GPUs dramatically improve image recognition.
  9. 2014GANs: Ian Goodfellow and collaborators introduce generative adversarial networks for producing synthetic data.
  10. 2016AlphaGo: DeepMind combines deep neural networks, search and reinforcement learning to defeat Lee Sedol 4–1.
  11. 2017Transformers: Google researchers introduce the attention-based architecture behind many modern language models.
  12. 2020–21Large models and science: GPT-3 shows large-scale language generation; AlphaFold 2 advances protein-structure prediction.
  13. 2022–23Generative AI goes mainstream: ChatGPT and GPT-4 bring conversational and multimodal AI to a global audience.
  14. 2024–26Multimodal, reasoning and agents: systems that can use tools and computers.

Why the winters happened, and what ended them

First winter, 1974–80

Overpromising, limited computing power, weak data resources and disappointing results. Public and government funding fell.

Second winter, 1987–93

Expensive specialised AI hardware, brittle expert systems and unmet commercial expectations. Companies failed or withdrew investment.

The revival

More digital data, faster processors and GPUs, improved algorithms, open-source tools, cloud computing and major investment.

Where AI is making a difference

Healthcare

Medical imaging support, drug discovery and protein-structure prediction.

Finance

Fraud detection, risk modelling, customer service and forecasting.

Agriculture

Crop monitoring, disease detection, yield prediction and precision farming.

Transport

Driver-assistance systems, route optimisation and autonomous-vehicle research.

Education and work

Tutoring support, translation, content creation, coding and workflow automation.

Energy and industry

Predictive maintenance, demand forecasting and optimisation of power systems.

Audience and presentation

I designed the timeline for colleagues and hiring managers who use AI tools but did not study them. That audience does not need every date in the field. It needs the shape of the story and a reason to care. So I grouped milestones into eras, marked the two winters as turning points, linked each era to a real-world use, and kept the language plain. A visual timeline lets a reader see the pattern in seconds, and the reference slide lets a sceptical reader check the facts.

Sources used in the deck

  • Anthropic: Model announcements (2024–2026)
  • Google Research: Attention Is All You Need (2017)
  • IBM: The History of Artificial Intelligence
  • Our World in Data: A Brief History of Artificial Intelligence

Value proposition

Unique value

I explained why AI progressed in waves, not only when. Overpromising and limited data and computing power caused the winters; more data, GPUs, better algorithms and cloud computing ended them. I also kept the distinction that today's systems are narrow AI and general AI remains an unachieved goal. This shows I can research a broad topic, find the pattern, and stay accurate instead of repeating hype.

Relevance

Analysts now work alongside AI tools, and the people they report to ask what those tools can and cannot do. Knowing where the tools came from helps me use them with sound judgment and explain them clearly to colleagues who are not technical. A resource like this timeline is useful to any team adopting AI, because it gives them shared background in a few minutes.
