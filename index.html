A1

AI and machine learning timeline, 1950–2026

Introduction

AI tools are now part of everyday analytics work, but they did not appear overnight. This artifact traces how the field got here, from the Turing Test to generative and agentic AI.

Description

A five-slide visual history of artificial intelligence and machine learning. It covers major milestones, influential researchers and technologies, the two AI winters and what ended them, where AI is making a difference today, and the sources behind the facts.

Objective

Show that progress in AI was not a straight line, and explain what data, algorithms and computing power made possible, so that a non-specialist can judge today's tools with more confidence.

Tools and technologies used

  • PowerPoint
  • Desk research
  • Timeline design
  • Source citation

Process

  1. Collected the major milestones from 1950 to 2026 and the researchers behind them.
  2. Arranged them into four eras: early symbolic rules, statistical learning, deep neural networks, then large-scale generative and agentic systems.
  3. Examined the first AI winter (1974–80) and second AI winter (1987–93): their causes, their impact, and what enabled the revival.
  4. Mapped real-world uses across healthcare, finance, agriculture, transport, education and work, and energy and industry.
  5. Added a reference slide listing leading researchers, AI companies and labs, and linked sources.

Key milestones

  1. 1950Turing Test: Alan Turing proposes the imitation game as a way to discuss machine intelligence.
  2. 1956Dartmouth: John McCarthy and colleagues establish AI as a formal research field.
  3. 1959Machine learning: Arthur Samuel popularises the term with a self-improving checkers program.
  4. 1974–80First AI winter: funding and optimism decline as early systems fall short of ambitious promises.
  5. 1986Backpropagation: Hinton, Rumelhart and Williams help popularise training multilayer neural networks.
  6. 1987–93Second AI winter: expert-system limits and a market downturn reduce investment and confidence.
  7. 1997Deep Blue: IBM's chess computer defeats world champion Garry Kasparov.
  8. 2012AlexNet: deep convolutional networks trained on GPUs dramatically improve image recognition.
  9. 2014GANs: Ian Goodfellow and collaborators introduce generative adversarial networks for producing synthetic data.
  10. 2016AlphaGo: DeepMind combines deep neural networks, search and reinforcement learning to defeat Lee Sedol 4–1.
  11. 2017Transformers: Google researchers introduce the attention-based architecture behind many modern language models.
  12. 2020–21Large models and science: GPT-3 shows large-scale language generation; AlphaFold 2 advances protein-structure prediction.
  13. 2022–23Generative AI goes mainstream: ChatGPT and GPT-4 bring conversational and multimodal AI to a global audience.
  14. 2024–26Multimodal, reasoning and agents: systems that can use tools and computers.

Why the winters happened, and what ended them

First winter, 1974–80

Overpromising, limited computing power, weak data resources and disappointing results. Public and government funding fell.

Second winter, 1987–93

Expensive specialised AI hardware, brittle expert systems and unmet commercial expectations. Companies failed or withdrew investment.

The revival

More digital data, faster processors and GPUs, improved algorithms, open-source tools, cloud computing and major investment.

Where AI is making a difference

Healthcare

Medical imaging support, drug discovery and protein-structure prediction.

Finance

Fraud detection, risk modelling, customer service and forecasting.

Agriculture

Crop monitoring, disease detection, yield prediction and precision farming.

Transport

Driver-assistance systems, route optimisation and autonomous-vehicle research.

Education and work

Tutoring support, translation, content creation, coding and workflow automation.

Energy and industry

Predictive maintenance, demand forecasting and optimisation of power systems.

Audience and presentation

I designed the timeline for colleagues and hiring managers who use AI tools but did not study them. That audience does not need every date in the field. It needs the shape of the story and a reason to care. So I grouped milestones into eras, marked the two winters as turning points, linked each era to a real-world use, and kept the language plain. A visual timeline lets a reader see the pattern in seconds, and the reference slide lets a sceptical reader check the facts.

Sources used in the deck

  • Anthropic: Model announcements (2024–2026)
  • Google Research: Attention Is All You Need (2017)
  • IBM: The History of Artificial Intelligence
  • Our World in Data: A Brief History of Artificial Intelligence

Value proposition

Unique value

I explained why AI progressed in waves, not only when. Overpromising and limited data and computing power caused the winters; more data, GPUs, better algorithms and cloud computing ended them. I also kept the distinction that today's systems are narrow AI and general AI remains an unachieved goal. This shows I can research a broad topic, find the pattern, and stay accurate instead of repeating hype.

Relevance

Analysts now work alongside AI tools, and the people they report to ask what those tools can and cannot do. Knowing where the tools came from helps me use them with sound judgment and explain them clearly to colleagues who are not technical. A resource like this timeline is useful to any team adopting AI, because it gives them shared background in a few minutes.

A2

AI lab: testing language models, STORM AI and a study chatbot

Introduction

AI tools give confident answers, but confident is not the same as correct or compliant. This lab tests three kinds of AI tool the way an analyst would: with criteria, with source checks and with planned test scenarios.

Description

A three-part lab. In Activity One I compared three ChatGPT responses to one prompt. In Activity Two I generated and audited an article on bias and fairness in machine learning with STORM AI. In Activity Three I designed, built and tested a study chatbot on Mizou.

Objective

Learn where AI tools are reliable and where they are not, and practise evaluating them and building with them responsibly.

Tools and technologies used

  • ChatGPT
  • STORM AI
  • Mizou
  • Prompt design
  • Scoring rubrics

Process

  1. Wrote one prompt with measurable constraints and ran it in three separate new chats.
  2. Scored the responses with a pass/fail constraint checklist and a 1 to 5 rubric.
  3. Generated an article with STORM AI, then checked its content, its brainstorming process and its citations.
  4. Planned a study chatbot with ten planning questions, built it in Mizou, and tested it against scenarios I had written beforehand.

Activity One: comparing three responses to one prompt

The prompt asked ChatGPT to explain how a recommendation system like Netflix or YouTube works: the data it collects, how it decides what to show, one limitation or risk, and one real-world example. I pasted it into three new chats and checked each response against my constraints.

What I found: all three covered the same four topics and were accurate, but none followed the word limit, the four-paragraph structure or the no-bullets rule. Chat 2 was the most informative and the easiest to fact-check, and also the longest. Depth and compliance pulled against each other, and no response admitted any uncertainty. I kept instruction-following as its own criterion because an answer can be accurate and complete and still fail the format rules. One chat is not enough to judge a model, and I would verify specific claims before relying on them.

Activity Two: auditing an AI-written article (STORM AI)

I chose bias and fairness in machine learning, a topic I know well enough to judge the output. STORM produced a full article in under three minutes, with a table of contents, a summary and 34 numbered references.

What worked

Comprehensive and well organised: sources of bias, legal frameworks, fairness notions, metrics, trade-offs, mitigation, case studies and future directions. Core concepts matched what I know, and nearly every sentence had a citation.

What I found wrong or thin

Only two real cases (COMPAS and FairTest) and almost no African or non-Western context. A medicines regulator named as "UK HMRA", which I believe should be MHRA. Thresholding listed under both pre-processing and post-processing. Statistical and social bias mixed together, with repeated points.

Source check

I opened citations to confirm that the numbered references existed and supported the paragraphs. The article leaned heavily on a few sources: [6], [7], [13] and [30].

How the tool seems to work: the brainstorming panel showed a simulated conversation between a writer, labelled "Basic fact writer", and topic experts. The writer asked three questions that built on each other, from bias versus fairness, to common fairness metrics, to the trade-offs between them, and each answer cited up to about 39 sources. The article followed the same order, so I think STORM gathers background, simulates questions and answers with citations, then organises the cited answers into an outline and writes each section. My view: it works as a starting outline and reading list, and I would verify claims and open the sources first.

Activity Three: a study chatbot on Mizou

I built a study buddy that quizzes students on data analysis with Excel. I limited it to one subject so it stays accurate and is easy to test, and wrote a one-page set of notes covering data cleaning, formulas, conditional functions, lookups, statistics, PivotTables, charts and common errors.

Design

Before building, I answered the lab's ten planning questions. The bot quizzes students and explains their mistakes. It uses only my notes, asks one question at a time, and ends with a score and a next study step.

Guardrails

It must not write essays, do assignments, invent facts, give medical or legal advice, or ask for personal information. I planned a test scenario for each rule.

Build

Mizou generated three options at University level, and I chose "Quiz: Excel Functions Revision" because it best matched my notes. I saved a screenshot of the first draft to compare with the final version.

Challenges and fixes: the free Chatbase plan required a bank account, so I switched to Mizou, which is free for students. The generated draft was generic, so I replaced its instructions with my own rules and shortened them to fit Mizou's length limit without dropping a rule. I added my notes as the knowledge source, then tested six scenarios: asking for a quiz, a question covered by the notes, a question outside the notes, a request to write my essay, deliberately wrong answers, and finishing a quiz to check the score summary.

Audience and presentation

I wrote this entry for hiring managers and teams that are starting to use AI. They do not need the full lab write-up. They want proof that I can judge an AI tool instead of trusting it, and that I can build a small one with rules. So the page shows the scoring tables, names the specific errors I found, and keeps the three activities short. The reader can see how I measured, what I found, and what I did about it.

Value proposition

Unique value

I measure AI instead of admiring it. I turned a vague question, "is this answer good?", into a checklist and a rubric, kept instruction-following separate from accuracy, and caught errors a quick read would miss, such as a misnamed regulator and a method listed in two places. I also designed a chatbot's limits before building it and tested each rule.

Relevance

Teams are adopting AI tools faster than they are learning to evaluate them. An analyst who can compare outputs, check sources and set clear boundaries for a tool helps an employer use AI with fewer surprises.
